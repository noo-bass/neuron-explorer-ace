ACE-Step 1.5 · renderer · layer 12 · how the ranking works

Is this neuron special, or would any nudge do that?

We pushed on hundreds of neurons inside a music model and measured what happened to the sound. This page walks through how we decide which ones stand out, one step at a time.

Step 1 · Where we are looking

A list of numbers carries the music so far

The part of ACE-Step we are studying is the renderer: the piece that turns a song plan into actual sound. The fast "turbo" version builds a 10-second clip in 8 passes, starting from noise and making it a little more like music each time.

Each pass runs the clip through a stack of 24 layers. Between one layer and the next, everything the model "knows" about the music at that moment travels as one long list of 2,048 numbers. We look at the list coming out of layer 12, the middle of the stack. If we change that list, everything after it has to work with the change.

Step 2 · What a push is

A push nudges the list along a pattern

Layer 12 has 6,144 neurons. Each neuron has its own pattern over the 2,048 numbers: a recipe like "raise this one a lot, lower that one a little, leave this one alone". When the neuron fires, it adds its pattern to the list.

To push neuron 5455, we add a little extra of its pattern by hand, on every pass. Push size +0.3 means the extra is about three-tenths as big as what normally flows through this layer; -0.3 is the same pattern, pushed the other way. Why do it this way? Because it asks the model directly: "if this neuron were more active, what would the music do?"

Step 3 · The comparison push

A random push: same size, no meaning

A random push is exactly the same size of nudge, but along a made-up pattern that belongs to no neuron. It is like turning a dial with no label on it. We made 256 of these.

Step 4 · Why bother with random pushes

Any nudge changes the music a bit

Poke a finely balanced machine anywhere and something will change. So "pushing neuron 5455 changed the song" on its own tells us nothing. The real question is: does the neuron change the music differently from a meaningless nudge of the same size?

The 256 random pushes show us what "nothing special" looks like. If a neuron's result sits inside the crowd of random results, we have no evidence it means anything. If it lands somewhere no random push ever reached, that is worth a closer look.

Step 5 · What we measured

Numbers about the sound, compared with the untouched song

For every pushed clip we worked out a few simple numbers about the sound and compared each one with the same song rendered with no push. Two of them matter on this page:

  • Overall change: how different the sound wave is from the untouched song. 0 means identical; bigger means more different. It cannot say what changed, only how much.
  • Note focus: how much of the sound sits on a few musical notes. A clear chord gives high focus; hiss or noise spreads across all notes and gives low focus. We record how much the push raised or lowered it.

Every push was tried on 4 songs: lo-fi with piano, a piano ballad, a rock band, and an ambient synth piece. We average the change across the 4, because a real effect should show up on more than one song, while a fluke on one song gets watered down.

Step 6 · The key chart

Overall change: neurons against random pushes

Each dot below is one push at +0.3, placed by its average overall change. The top row is the 256 random pushes; the bottom row is 256 neurons. The shaded strip is the territory random pushes cover: its right edge is the random push that changed the music most, and its left edge is the same distance on the other side of the random average.

Why this is convincing: if neurons were no different from random directions, they would be drawn from the same crowd, and about none of them would land beyond the most extreme random push. Instead, dozens do, and some land two or three times further out than any random push ever went.

Step 8 · A caution

"Changes more" is not "does one clear thing"

Beating every random push tells us a neuron has an unusually strong grip on the sound. It does not tell us the grip does anything musical. Some of the top neurons might simply scramble the sound harder than a random nudge can.

There is a hint of this in the top five below: three of them (3905, 1675, 4933) are also the biggest raisers of note focus, meaning the sound collapsed onto a few notes. That could be a held drone rather than a musical idea. Nobody has listened to them yet.

So these crude numbers catch how much changes, not yet what changes. Taking out one instrument can be a small change to the whole sound wave and still be the most meaningful thing in the list.
